### Main Function(s) + Requirements

<br />

The stirring mechanism in the bioreactor uses a brushless DC fan as a low cost rotating motor. Creating a rotating magnetic field, two small nickel-plated neodymium magnets secured to the fan hub with Gorilla glue; a magnetic stir bar housed inside the vial follows this field and mixes all components suspended with it. Following the DBLT cycle, initially, several requirements like low power consumption, cost efficiency, and providing various speed settings. However, throughout testing, and by better understanding stakeholder needs, specifically the bacteria used in experiments, we identified the most critical requirement: limiting the fan speed. It was found that the lowest RPM of the acting prototype, 1800 RPM, would risk harming the bacteria, while too low a voltage may not start the fan at all. Throughout iterations of this subsystem, speed control at a known RPM, and reduced below 800 was prioritized throughout this subsystem’s design process. Within the context of the entire vessel, the strength of the magnetic field through the heating system was also considered.

### Build, Assembly, Code

We first started with outlining many requirements for the fan drawn from competitor research, main requirements were outlined as: starting voltage, 3 or 4 pin fan, footprint of 9x6 [cm^2], reduction control from 0-100% range, and under 5$.

The Sunon MF40201V1-1000U-G99 was selected for its low starting voltage range (except the range was very large, 4.5–5.5 VDC) and footprint. It was low cost and sourced from [DigiKey](https://www.digikey.ca/en/products/detail/sunon-fans/MF40201V1-1000U-G99/7927780).

After wiring the fan in several different layouts: directly from nano, using tachometer pins diodes and mosfets, and assembling the magnets and vial with make-shift housing, the magnetic field seemed strong enough to create a vortex in water with the stir bar, and strong enough to pass through the heater. It was found that upon attempting to control the speed of the fan, however, the
fan’s original speed was 6000 RPM, and despite different strategies like using
the tachometer pin to make the fan slow down, using a transistor, and adding
many resistors to the power wire, the floor RPM the fan would spin was 1800RPM.
As outlined in the introduction, this rate was too high, and risked damaging
bacteria.

Fan selection resumed, searching for a new fan.
