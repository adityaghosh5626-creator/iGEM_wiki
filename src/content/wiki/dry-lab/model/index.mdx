## Motivation

{/*
  ~200 words. Answers Q1. No figure.
  Why a model is needed at all: the question it has to answer.
*/}

<Placeholder>

Known PET-degrading enzymes are few, and each one took years of bench work to find and characterise. Public sequence databases hold far more candidate enzymes than any lab could ever test, so the limiting step is choosing which ones to make.

We built a model to make that choice. Given a sequence that has never been expressed, it estimates how likely the enzyme is to break down plastic, so that wet lab effort goes to the candidates most likely to work.

</Placeholder>

## What Our Model Does

{/*

<Placeholder>

Our model ranks candidate enzymes by predicted plastic-degrading activity. That ranking decided which sequences wet lab ordered and tested, and the assay results feed back in as new training data.

It is built from four components, each described in its own section below:

- **Dataset Generation** builds the corpus of candidate sequences the model ranks.
- **Sequence Annotation** describes each sequence with the features the model can learn from.
- **Activity Data Extraction** collects measured activity from the literature as training labels.
- **Machine Learning** learns from the labelled sequences and scores the rest of the corpus.

</Placeholder>

## Dataset Generation

{/*

<Placeholder>

The corpus is the set of sequences the model scores. We start from the enzymes already reported to degrade plastic, curated and reconciled across the public reference databases, and group them into families by sequence similarity.

From each family we search outward, first into the NCBI protein database and then into assembled public sequencing data through Logan, using profile models that require the catalytic residues to be present. The sequences recovered are clustered at several identity thresholds so the corpus can be sampled without redundancy.

</Placeholder>

Annotation supplies the features the model learns from. A raw sequence says little on its own, so we attach to each one what can be inferred about it: the environment its source sample came from, including growth temperature and pH; its domain architecture; its predicted three-dimensional structure; and where in the cell it is likely to be sent.

Each layer is computed across the corpus by its own pipeline, and coverage differs from layer to layer. We report that coverage here because a feature the model cannot see for most sequences cannot inform most predictions.

</Placeholder>

### Localization and Signal Peptides

SignalP 6.0 was run on all PETadex ORFs to identify the secreted enzymes within the database, to support enzyme selection for in vitro activity testing and to produce cleaved mature sequences for more accurate downstream analysis. Bacterial enzymes primarily employ the Sec (Secretory) and Tat (Twin-Arginine Translocation) pathways, which export proteins before and after they complete folding respectively. SignalP classifies 5 signal peptide classes within the two pathways (SP, LIPO, TAT, TATLIPO, and PILIN). Overall, 13.85% of PETadex sequences were predicted to contain a signal peptide, within which 92.21% of the enzymes have Sec routing and 7.79% have Tat. 90% identity enzyme clusters had on average 93.1% agreement on whether its member sequences is signal peptide-positive, and 98.3% of these positive members shared the same signal peptide class. The gap in agreement within 90% clusters is most likely due to missing N-termini from sequence fragmentation, making SignalP a useful quality check on the database. Alpha/Beta Hydrolases had a below-average percentage of signal peptide-positive sequences (10.6%) but contributed 44% of all predicted secreted ORFs.

### Domain Annotation

*Owner: Nino*

Using the complete sequences from the input domains, InterProScan is used to determine
all domains located within the sequence. Then, using PETadex HMMs, HMMer (a tool that
searches through protein sequences to find domains corresponding to inputted HMMs)
searches through the sequences to determine the location of PET-relevant domains in the
sequences.
Now the coordinates of the HMMer results are overlayed on the Interproscan results, and
any domains that hit in both HMMer and Interproscan results are NOT accessory domains,
so they are filtered out. Two resulting datasets are outputted. One dataset containing all its
(including overlapping hits) from interproscan and HMMer and another dataset containing
only accessory domains.

### Biochemical Properties and Ancestral Reconstruction

Six sequence-derived properties were calculated using Biopython ProtParam for all PETadex ORFs: length, molecular weight, isoelectric point (pI), GRAVY hydrophobicity, instability index, and aromaticity. Median values were 308 aa, 34.2 kDa, pI 6.45, GRAVY −0.165, instability 37.5, and aromaticity 0.0885. Compared with a similarly-sized random sample of NCBI nr, certain property medians were generally similar (such as 289 aa, GRAVY −0.19), which strengthens PETadex being a database of real functional protein sequences. Additionally, PETadex was far narrower in GRAVY (standard deviation 0.20 vs 0.43), and had fewer comparably short, very long, or highly unstable sequences. This depletion of hydrophobic and extreme sequences is consistent with soluble catalytic domains rather than membrane proteins, supporting PETadex as a promising database for identifying plastic degrading enzymes. Mature secreted proteins were also more hydrophilic than non-secreted proteins, and catalytic-family median GRAVY correlated strongly with secretion (Spearman ρ = −0.96). Thus the properties serve as a useful filter for sequence selection as well.

### LLM Extraction Pipeline

To rapidly and accurately aggregate plastic-degrading enzyme data from academic literature, we developed an automated, high-precision extraction pipeline. Rather than relying solely on manual human curation (as is the case in other databases like PAZY) this pipeline leverages large language models (LLMs) to parse through 1000s of dense academic PDFs. The system is designed to find activity data on true plastic-degrading enzymes that were tested in primary papers ensuring the database is populated only with experimentally validated targets. This process involves a two-stage pipeline to extract relevant values, followed by numerous sanitation and verification steps to remove potential hallucinations and recover missing data. 
Methods: 
Articles were scraped using APIs and automated web-crawlers. An initial pool of plastic degrading papers were found using keyword searches and metadata accessions from PETadex sequences, then this pool was expanded by scraping papers cited by those papers, and papers that cited them. A combination of text extraction tools including Docling, Grobid and PyMuPDF were used to extract sectioned text from these pdfs, as well as convert figures into based encoded text. The relevancy of these papers were assessed using an LLM to remove papers with no activity data on plastic degrading enzymes. An extraction pipeline was built leveraging Claude Sonnet via AWS bedrock. The development of this pipeline came over several iterations of tests against synthetic controls and benchmarking experiments. Firstly synthetic controls were created using an LLM and human verifier to inject synthetic protein and assay data into 200 unique papers, these proteins included real plastic degrading enzymes with plausible activity data and decoy proteins that were not plastic degrading enzymes, then their capture and rejection rates were tested along the pipeline. Benchmarking experiments were run by comparing the outputs of Sonnet, Haiku, Opus and hybrid combinations (ie pass 1 using opus and pass 2 using sonnet) with each other to optimize the model used from those available. Finally results have been compared against human extracted data. Each of these testing steps were used to gauge the performance of the pipeline and improve on it for the next iteration.
Results: 
The final extraction pipeline involved 8 LLM steps and 3 non-LLM steps costing around 33 cents/paper: 
Pass 1: Sonnet 4.6 generates a dossier of all the plastic degrading enzymes in the paper and the experiments those enzymes participated with the corresponding substrates in using chronological thinking (followed steps while outputting “thinking”, ie: producing a full list of proteins, then eliminating the non-plastic ones, etc).                                                                                                  
Pass 2: using the dossier from pass 1 Sonnet 4.6 extracts the relevant protein and activity data along with the exact verbatim evidence sentence the value was pulled from.
Verification step 1: Using regex to check if the verbatim evidence sentences truly originated from the papers or were hallucinated
Verification step 2: Using simple python, the enzyme-substrate combinations from the dossier in pass 1 is compared to the extracted list in pass 2, any new proteins are flagged for review (making it easier for later LLM steps will check) and any missing proteins are added in a notes section for LLM review
Verification step 3: A list of substrates is generated and compared against an exhaustive list of substrates to lightly flag any substrates not found in the list (making it easier for later LLM steps will check)
Verification step 4: Sonnet 4.6 is given a list of the proteins extracted and their substrates and checks to see if they are actually tested on plastic substrates. Removes any proteins that aren’t with associated protein data.                                                                              
Rescue step 4.1: Sonnet 4.6 checks if any proteins that are tested against plastic/rubber substrates, are not mentioned in the list, adds them if they are
Verification step 5: Sonnet 4.6 is given the extracted values, the variable type (temp, ph, etc) and their associated evidence sentences to check if the values are correct and originate from the evidence sentence, the LLM will replace any incorrect values with either the correct value (if present in the evidence sentence) or “Not_Found”
Rescue step 5.1: Sonnet 4.6 is given the full papers and the extracted values that have “Not_Found” in the list, and looks for their actual values and an accompanying evidence sentence, verification step 4 is rerun on these sentences.
Verification step 6: Sonnet 4.6 is given the full paper and groups of variables and proteins that are associated with each other, then asked to ensure that each “relational pool” is correctly associated. Any values that aren’t are flagged.
Rescue step 6.1: Sonnet 4.6 takes the flagged values and associates them with the correct relational pool                                                                         
When tested against synthetic controls the final version of the pipeline scored high:                          
100% decoy rejection rate (no false positives) 
99.3% (152/153) Valid Spike-in Capture Rate (true positives)
100% (152/152) Relational linkage Assays and Proteins 
98.8% (712/720) Assay Variable Extraction Rate 
98.0% (196/200) Activity Metric Extraction Rate 
When benchmarked against the other anthropic models Sonnet outperformed the other models with 95.0% Precision, 87.1% Recall, 90.9% F1 Score, 97.7% Global Metric Accuracy. Haiku: 90.1% Precision, 81.5% Recall, 85.6% F1 Score, 94.4% Global Metric Accuracy. Opus: 100.0% Precision, 9.1% Recall, 16.7% F1 Score, 98.1% Global Metric Accuracy (severely limited by under-extraction). And while the proteins extracted by the models varied, the proteins the models did agree on extracted the exact same data with minor formatting differences.
An initial run of this pipeline on ~4k papers yielded 27,244 experiments, 31,441 enzyme records (after merging 419 duplicates) and 61,771 measurement rows. PET and related compounds make up 32% of retained measurements and aliphatic polyesters (PCL, PLA, PHA, PBS) another 29%. Polyolefins are 7%, polyurethane 5% and polystyrene 4%. The most measured enzymes are Proteinase K, laccase, LCC-ICCG, PETase, IsPETase and HiC. About 19% of enzyme records are engineered mutants.


### Schema Design and Extraction Protocol

In addition to an automated scraping pipeline, 20+ scrapers spent 450+ hours manually extracting data from 60+ plastic-degradation papers, formatting the data through a standardized interactive HTML schema. The schema captures each paper’s enzymes, experimental variables, setup, and individual activity measurements, then exports the data into structured CSVs. This manually curated dataset provides ground truth for benchmarking LLM-based literature extraction and creates a more filterable resource for future plastic-degradation research.

The schema was created under 3 guiding principles: 1) Standardization in variables, experiment conditions, and units via dropdown selections to support effective data analysis and transferability to LLM guidelines. 2) Structure & Traceability, where autogenerated experiment and enzyme IDs let every extracted data point be backsourced. 3) Guardrailing with built-in error checks to reduce human errors and missing data entries.

Every paper was also independently extracted by two or more scrapers, with 78% of the corresponding raw un-reconciled data agreeing within 10%, which supports the schema's strength as an organizer. However, a non-trivial portion of data entries have no matches between pairs of scrapers as a result of scraper(s) overlooking sections of the papers. This showcases how even non-automated pipelines for data extraction are fallible to errors, lending strength to the potential benefits of a rigorous LLM-based model

### Human vs LLM Agreement

Finally when checking 26 LLM extracted papers against 26 double-human verified papers large discrepancies remain both between the LLM-Human comparisons and Human-Human comparisons. The LLM found 70% of enzymes either human listed (a second human finds 83%) and 90% of those both humans agreed on. Only 7 of 180 LLM enzyme names had no human match, and those were naming variants or whole-cell entries. When the enzyme and value match, the LLM's pH agrees 80% of the time (a second human: 91%) and buffer 75% (84%). The errors are usually a condition borrowed from another assay in the same paper, like pH 7 versus 8 for IsPETase, or 37 versus 50 °C for Dkca1. Temperature (92%) and plasmid (95%) are good.

### Embedding Controls

Using the ESMC-XXXM Protein Language Model (PLM), we generated embeddings for X 30%pid PETadex centroids. These X dimensional embeddings were then UMAPed to 2 dimensions using the umap-learn (https://umap-learn.readthedocs.io/en/latest/) Python library, resulting in Figure 1.


<Figure
  src="https://static.igem.wiki/teams/6187/wiki/drylab-page/figures/umap-cath.avif"
  alt="UMAPed enzymes, coloured by CATH domain"
/>

Noticing apparent clusters by CATH domains in the UMAP (See Figure 1), we ran further analysis to characterize these clusters, and investigate outliers. Silhouette scores found that most CATH domains were indeed highly clustered, minus Alpha/Beta Hydrolases, but since these make up ~⅔ of the PETadex, we would not expect them to be as tightly clustered. The distribution of silhouette scores per CATH domain is shown in Figure 2.

<Figure
  src="https://static.igem.wiki/teams/6187/wiki/drylab-page/figures/silhouette.avif"
  alt="Silhouette scores per CATH domain"
/>

Notebook: 
https://github.com/petadex/igemtoronto/blob/main/notebooks/260324_issue51_clusterquantification.ipynb (issue #51) 

Finally, we ran controls on this UMAP to verify that this clustering does not appear by chance. Our objectives are the following: 
Generate negative controls (random and shuffled sequences) from the 64,730 family representatives, designed to disentangle amino-acid composition from sequence order.
Generate positive / real-world controls (random UniProt proteins, real-sequence fragments) for comparison against the real centroids.


Controls are then embedded identically to the real proteins, and UMAPed alongside them, as seen in Figure 3. We found that the shuffled sequences and random/matched/empirical form their own "junkyard" regions, far away from PETadex sequences, Uniprot embeddings are scattered randomly, and fragments hug the real data closely. These results confirmed that the clustering seen in our Atlas reflected real signal and meaning in ESMC embeddings.
