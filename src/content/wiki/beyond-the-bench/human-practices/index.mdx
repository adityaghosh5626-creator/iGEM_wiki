## Executive Overview

Plastic pollution does not stop at borders and neither should the solution. A technology that works only in a well-funded Canadian treatment plant solves a narrow slice of the problem for the communities that can already afford infrastructure. We built PetaBite around that constraint. From the start, we set a moonshot goal of building a workflow that produces highly efficient plastic-degrading enzymes for any set of operating conditions, rather than optimizing a single enzyme for a single environment.

So we pursued that goal on two fronts: listening to the people our technology would affect and opening synthetic biology to people who have never had access to it.

To hold ourselves to that standard, we produced two formal reports.

### Regulatory Oversight

The Regulatory Oversight Framework maps what it would actually take to deploy a biological solution responsibly. It covers our open science obligations under Canada's Roadmap for Open Science and the FAIR principles, benefit-sharing duties under the Nagoya Protocol for enzyme sequences drawn from biodiversity-rich nations, dual-use research of concern, and the AI governance frameworks that apply to PETadex, including PIPEDA, the Directive on Automated Decision-Making, and the EU AI Act.

On the biosafety side, the regulatory framework examines our project under the Canadian Environmental Protection Act and includes country‑specific profiles for Pakistan, India, Nigeria, Indonesia, and Canadian Indigenous communities, developed from primary stakeholder interviews. These profiles evaluate the ethics of deploying a Global North technology in regions disproportionately affected by plastic pollution, addressing waste colonialism, ethics dumping, and the extent to which open access enables genuine usability in areas with limited infrastructure and technical capacity.

### Environmental and Sustainability Considerations

The Sustainability Report evaluates whether our solution mitigates existing problems without creating new ones. It measures our environmental footprint across the energy demands of the AI pipeline, enzyme synthesis and production, hardware use, biological and chemical waste streams, and water consumption. It also includes carbon emission calculations for our Dry Lab, Wet Lab, and Hardware operations.

Beyond environmental metrics, the report addresses microplastic fate, enzyme containment, and integration within a circular economy. On the social side, it examines cost and accessibility, public perception of engineered enzymes and AI‑driven databases across cultural and regional contexts, and outlines an ethical deployment framework specifying the evidence required before release. We close with recommendations for the field and directions for future research.

### Stakeholder Outreach

These reports shaped our technical decisions as much as our outreach. We learned from <strong>~70 stakeholders</strong> this season through various interviews, conferences and competitions, including researchers, regulators, ethicists, conference panelists, plant operators, Indigenous knowledge holders and industry experts across four continents. Those conversations changed where we deploy, what evidence we gather before deployment, and how we frame enzymatic degradation alongside prevention and policy.

### Education

On the education side, we hosted <strong>three conferences</strong>, mentored <strong>two high school teams</strong> in synthetic biology, ran sustained exchanges with <strong>four universities abroad</strong>, and carried out <strong>eight campus collaborations</strong> across the University of Toronto that reached over <strong>800</strong> students and community members cumulatively.

## Commitment to Inclusivity

Solving a global challenge requires input from the people who will be most affected by the solution. We embedded this principle throughout our project by building it into our recruitment practices, laboratory culture, and engagement with advisors, stakeholders, and community members.

<Dropdown title="Holistic stakeholder engagement">

We engaged every group our technology affects. Plant operators described how facilities function in practice. Regulators at Environment and Climate Change Canada and the U.S. EPA mapped the approval pathways we must navigate. Community organizations such as the U of T Trash Team challenged our framing. Indigenous knowledge holders reframed plastic pollution as a matter of relationships among people, land, and water rather than a purely technical issue.

</Dropdown>

<Dropdown title="Inclusive team composition">

Our seven subteams include members from molecular biology, computational biology, machine learning, hardware engineering, environmental science, and business. New members join through an open application process with no prior lab experience required because we found that experience requirements often exclude the students who most need the opportunity.

</Dropdown>

<Dropdown title="Inclusive lab culture">

All members review a code of conduct at the start of the season. We train new members and we structure subteam work so first‑year students have the opportunity to present alongside senior members.

</Dropdown>

## Our Mission

### The Fragmented Crisis

It is hard to believe a solution to any problem can be achieved when its solvers are fragmented. Scale this to a crisis that affects every continent on Earth, and the ability to act efficiently and ethically can seem impossible. Plastic pollution is one such crisis; from the deep oceans to the human placenta, this pressing issue has proven to be immensely complex.

As a result, multidisciplinary action united against this threat is essential for any sustainable solution. However, this endeavor is remarkably difficult because the global plastics community is deeply disjointed, which heavily reduces efficiency in creating practical interventions.

### The Two Pillars of PetaBite

Human Practices presented a unique opportunity to bring together all sectors of the plastics community. To make the optimal, lasting difference, we realized a true solution must stand on two equal pillars:

1. **A robust, scalable technical solution** - leveraging engineering to solve the physical presence of plastic pollution around the world.
2. **A unified community platform** that bridges the disjointed plastics landscape by uniting data scientists, policy-makers, plant operators, ethicists, and local communities.

### The Iterative Feedback Loop

Our work this year used global stakeholder engagement to analyze the real-world challenges our project would encounter in unique contexts around the world. This created an iterative process that directly shaped our technical solutions and inspired us to build multidisciplinary frameworks to address the concerns raised in our interviews.

Using the core themes that emerged from these stakeholder sessions, our work bringing together the global plastics community culminated in the <strong>MERGE</strong> framework:

<Dropdown title="M — Multidisciplinary Global Stakeholder Outreach">

We spoke with ~70 stakeholders across four continents, this included plant operators, regulators, ethicists, and Indigenous knowledge holders. Our interviews aimed to ensure our technology actually fit real-world needs.

</Dropdown>

<Dropdown title="E — Education and Multi-Directional Feedback">

We broke down academic silos by hosting three conferences, mentoring high school teams, running international university exchanges, and reaching over 800 students through campus collaborations.

</Dropdown>

<Dropdown title="R — Regulatory Oversight">

We mapped out legal and safety realities, navigating global plastic, AI, and PETase regulations as well as considerations about open science and ethics to bring together one framework allowing crosstalk to show how plastic solutions would fit into global contexts.

</Dropdown>

<Dropdown title="G — Global Consolidated Database Interviews">

We created the <strong>PETadex</strong>, a database that summarizes years of work from the plastics community, establishing a central hub where research is globally accessible to a traditionally disjointed field.

</Dropdown>

<Dropdown title="E — Environmental & Sustainability Consideration">

We reflected on our projected life-cycle footprint (covering energy, carbon, water, and waste) while addressing microplastic fate and biological containment.

</Dropdown>
This framework successfully guided the execution of our project this year. Beyond PetaBite, the <strong>MERGE</strong> framework serves as a scalable model for any future initiative that aims to uphold our two core pillars: developing a robust technical solution to synthetic biology while actively unifying the scientific community.

## Multidisciplinary Global Stakeholder Outreach

### Executive Overview

When we began the journey of seeking stakeholders, we knew we couldn’t only feature people who have historically had a seat at the table. We identified communities that are most affected by plastics, including Nigeria, Pakistan, Indonesia, and the Indigenous communities of Canada, to understand how our solution could fit into the unique environments and realities of these locations. This is because PetaBite's goal was always to follow the 2 pillars we identified for the best solution. Not only a great technical solution, but one that was built around the entire Plastics community.
We organized our interviews into two streams, each designed to address a distinct category of questions.

### Industry and Community Interviews

These conversations tested both the feasibility and the ethics of deploying our technology beyond the laboratory. We spoke with wastewater treatment plant operators and environmental scientists about operational viability, with bioethicists and regulatory specialists about the governance structures a biological deployment would encounter, and with Indigenous scholars about environmental stewardship and our responsibilities to the communities and ecosystems affected by our work.

Several of these interviews directly informed our <strong>Regulatory Framework</strong> and <strong>Sustainability Report</strong>.
