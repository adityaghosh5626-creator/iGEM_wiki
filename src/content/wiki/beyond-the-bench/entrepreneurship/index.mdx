## 1. Executive Summary

PetaBite and PetaScale share a single ambition: transforming microplastic biodegradation from a student‑led research initiative into a commercially viable platform capable of global scale. **PetaBite**, our iGEM project, served as the technical proving ground, taking the technology and prototype from 0 to 1. **PetaScale**, our commercialization effort, now builds on that foundation to carry it from 1 to n, translating the validated science into real‑world deployment and industrial partnerships.

We tested and refined our business model across **five Canadian national competitions, four global competitions, and two international conferences, with support from 17 institutional and community sponsors - the most in iGEM Toronto history.** In **six** of those competitions, **we placed within the top 3 overall,** standing out from a cumulative field of hundreds of early‑stage startups and university design teams.

The same enzymatic platform supports all four verticals.

## 2. Problem Statement

Global plastic production has grown far beyond what waste systems were built to handle. Annual output rose from about 1.5 million tonnes in 1950 to more than 430 million tonnes in 2024, a nearly 290-fold increase<Citation number={4} />, and the trajectory shows no sign of reversing. Under the OECD's baseline scenario, global plastics use is projected to grow from 460 million tonnes in 2019 to 1,231 million tonnes by 2060, outpacing most raw materials<Citation number={5} />. Over the same period, annual plastic leakage into the environment is projected to double from about 22 million tonnes to 44 million tonnes, and the stock of plastic in aquatic ecosystems is projected to more than triple, from about 140 million tonnes to nearly 500 million tonnes<Citation number={5} />. Much of this plastic breaks down into microplastics, particles smaller than 5 mm that persist for centuries under environmental conditions and spread through soil, waterways, food and drinking water.

### Effects on Human Health

Unfortunately, evidence of microplastics in the human body continues to grow. Researchers have detected micro- and nanoplastics in human blood<Citation number={6} />, placenta<Citation number={7} />, and organs including the kidney, liver, and brain. Concentrations in brain tissue rose by about 50% between 2016 and 2024 and were highest in patients with dementia<Citation number={8} />. Cardiovascular data raise similar concerns. Patients whose carotid artery plaque contained microplastics had about 4.5 times the risk of heart attack, stroke or death over the following three years compared with patients whose plaque did not<Citation number={9} />.

### Effects on Agriculture

Microplastics also threaten agriculture. Microplastics that build up in soil can disrupt plant metabolism, reduce crop yields, and harm soil organisms<Citation number={10} />. The smallest particles can also enter plants directly. Crop plants absorb submicrometre plastics through their roots and move them into stems and leaves, including edible tissue such as lettuce leaves<Citation number={11} />. This creates a potential pathway for plastic particles to enter the food chain through the consumption of contaminated crops. Plastics in soil can also carry pathogens. When plastic surfaces come into contact with wastewater or livestock waste, microbial biofilms colonize them, and this community, known as the plastisphere, can harbour human pathogens and antibiotic resistance genes<Citation number={12} />. Because microplastics stick to crop surfaces and washing does not fully remove them, they may carry these pathogens onto food<Citation number={13} />.

### The Status Quo

Current approaches to plastic waste include membrane filtration, mechanical recycling, chemical recycling, and incineration, with each having major limits. Filtration captures microplastics and moves them into sludge or waste streams without breaking them down<Citation number={87} /><Citation number={143} />. Mechanical recycling degrades material quality with each cycle<Citation number={106} />. Incineration releases emissions and destroys the material. Chemical recycling can break PET into its monomers, terephthalic acid (TPA) and ethylene glycol (EG), and rebuild them into resin<Citation number={106} />, but that resin costs more than fossil-derived plastic, which limits its commercial viability. None of these approaches removes microplastics already in the environment at a cost that works at scale. The world needs a way to break plastic down into its molecular building blocks at the points where it accumulates.

### Regulatory Pressure

As a result, regulators across major markets now treat microplastics as a compliance issue. The EU Regulation 2023/2055 restricts the use of intentionally added synthetic polymer microparticles<Citation number={14} />, while Directive 2024/3019 requires member states to monitor microplastics in urban wastewater treatment systems<Citation number={15} />. In Canada, the Federal Plastics Registry mandates that manufacturers and importers report detailed data on the plastics they place on the market<Citation number={16} />. Since 2022, more than 170 countries have been negotiating a global treaty to end plastic pollution<Citation number={17} />.

### The Timing of PetaScale

Thus, the timing for PetaScale’s plastic degrading enzyme (PETase) technology is exceptionally strong. Governments are tightening compliance frameworks, and industries managing plastic waste urgently need scalable biological solutions to a problem that has persisted for decades. Advances in enzyme engineering and metagenomic discovery have brought enzymatic degradation closer to commercial viability. The scale of the challenge, its human and agricultural costs, coupled with mounting regulatory pressure, has created a market where technical advantage is both valuable and defensible. PetaBite/PetaScale meets this need through a scalable biological platform for PET microplastic removal, a solution explored in detail in the following sections.

## 3. Solution / Technology Overview

Plastic-degrading enzymes (PETases) are a promising solution to microplastic pollution and engineered variants such as FAST-PETase and LCC-ICCG already perform well under controlled lab conditions. However, two limitations still hold the field back: a lack of data and poor performance outside the lab. Most groups improve the same handful of known enzymes through rounds of engineering, which leaves most of nature's enzyme diversity untested. These enzymes also only work best under very narrow temperature, pH, and substrate concentration ranges. LCC-ICCG, one of the best-performing variants, needs temperatures near 70 °C<Citation number={1} />, which makes it impractical in wastewater treatment plants, lakes and mixed waste streams. Wet lab testing is also slow, and while unlabeled sequence data is abundant, the field has very little labelled data on how enzymes perform across real operating conditions. Progress has therefore concentrated on optimizing a few known scaffolds for controlled reactor conditions, which leaves performance in the field largely unaddressed.

**Our mission is to deploy the most efficient and versatile plastic-degrading enzymes at planetary scale.** Our approach differs from existing work in two ways: we search nature's full enzyme diversity and we design and test every enzyme for the specific environmental conditions where it will be used.

<Figure
  src="https://static.igem.wiki/teams/6187/wiki/venture/figures/3-solution-overview-figure.avif"
  alt="solution"
/>

We have already solved the first limitation: a lack of data. Before our work, researchers had characterized only about 495 plastic-degrading enzymes worldwide, far too few to train a model on which sequences degrade plastic and under what conditions. So our Dry Lab team built PETadex (petadex.net), the world's largest open-source database of plastic-degrading enzyme candidates, to close that gap. Using the Logan index, a planetary-scale metagenomic search system built from our associate lab, the RNAlab identified about 300M unique proteins from environments worldwide, expanding the searchable pool more than 600,000-fold. The first screen of 161 randomly selected candidates returned two that outperformed FAST-PETase, a leading engineered benchmark, which confirms that the database contains enzymes worth finding. Finally, we made the PETadex open access so any researcher can build on this diversity, since proprietary datasets and siloed efforts have slowed the field.

This iGEM cycle, we are solving the second limitation: performance outside the lab. Even the best PETases lose much of their activity in industrial conditions, at variable temperatures or alongside mixed contaminants. We are building a machine learning pipeline that uses the PETadex dataset to select and engineer enzymes for specific real-world conditions, starting with room-temperature activity. We test each candidate with high-throughput wet lab assays and an in-house bioreactor array that replicates the conditions enzymes face during deployment. Each round of testing feeds labelled data back into our models, so every cycle improves the next set of predictions. No other PETase effort we know of combines this scale of candidate discovery with systematic, condition-specific testing.

Our first target is wastewater treatment plants (WWTPs), where our enzymes can degrade PET at ambient temperatures. We will then expand to landfill leachate and contaminated bioremediation sites, where PETases can break down accumulated PET in water and soil. We also see opportunities at the points where PET first enters the environment: degrading PET fragments shed during textile manufacturing, clearing PET microfibres from washing machine filters and dryer lint traps<Citation number={59} /><Citation number={64} />, and formulating PETases into detergents that degrade microfibres released from synthetic fabrics during the wash cycle<Citation number={48} /><Citation number={61} />.

## 4. Value Proposition

Every company commercializing plastic degrading enzymes today builds around one engineered enzyme and one operating window. Companies such as Carbios<Citation number={18} />, Samsara Eco<Citation number={19} />, and Protein Evolution<Citation number={20} /> use enzymes to recycle concentrated, pre-sorted plastic inside controlled reactors, where they can heat the material, adjust pH and pretreat the plastic to suit the enzyme. Carbios, for example, grinds and amorphizes PET before degrading it near 70 °C. That model may work in a recycling plant, but it ultimately fails where microplastics actually accumulate. Heating the large volumes of water that flow through a wastewater treatment plant would be far too expensive, and conditions change from one part of a plant to the next: clarifiers run at ambient temperature, mesophilic anaerobic digesters run at about 35 °C, and thermophilic digesters run at about 55 °C.

PetaScale, on the other hand, matches the enzyme to the site. For each deployment point, we select and engineer an enzyme that works optimally at that point's existing temperature, pH and salinity, so the facility needs no heating, pretreatment or new infrastructure. This keeps retrofit costs low and lets one bioengineering platform serve multiple WWTPs, landfill leachates, facilities producing textile effluents, and appliance filtration environments.

Our competitive advantage is a growing portfolio of patented optimized enzymes, each engineered for a specific real-world condition. Engineered enzymes such as FAST-PETase and LCC-ICCG come from optimizing a small handful of known enzymes, which limits how far any design can extend beyond its original operating window. Our approach starts from a much larger set of natural sequences that evolved across a wide range of environments, some of which already compete with the best engineered enzymes, such as when the two top candidates from our first screen produced about four times more TPA than FAST-PETase under identical conditions. From these starting points, we engineer variants for each deployment condition and profile them on our bioreactor array across temperature, pH and salinity. Field data from every customer deployment then guides the next round of engineering. Although the PETadex may stay open to all researchers, the engineered enzymes we build on top of it are our own, and we patent each one. Every new deployment site and every new vertical adds more protected enzymes to our portfolio, so our lead widens as we grow.

## 5. Customer Segments

PetaScale serves municipal and industrial organizations that need a scalable way to remove microplastics from their water and waste streams.

**Beachhead market: wastewater treatment plants (WWTPs).** WWTPs are our most technically validated and commercially promising entry point. Wastewater from homes and industry flows through these plants before it reaches lakes and rivers, which makes them the first point where operators can control microplastics before they spread downstream. We immobilize our PETases in packed-bed reactors<Citation number={65} /><Citation number={99} />, which degrade PET polymers inside the plant while keeping the enzyme contained. Through 20 interviews with operators, researchers, and technical staff across Ontario, Alberta, and Newfoundland, we confirmed that our approach fits existing plant workflows. Purchasing decisions in this segment involve municipal infrastructure directors, plant operators and environmental compliance officers. Regulatory pressure on these decision-makers is rising, led by the EU's new requirement to monitor microplastics in urban wastewater treatment<Citation number={15} />.

### Beyond WWTPs, we have also explored expanding into three adjacent verticals.

**Textile effluent.** Textile manufacturers release PET fibres and fragments into their wastewater during production. PETases add an in-line treatment step that degrades this PET before the wastewater leaves the facility, with no change to the production process. Sustainability officers and production managers are the buyers in this segment and the treatment step supports their compliance and ESG reporting.

**Landfill leachate.** As plastic breaks down in landfills, it releases PET microplastics into leachate<Citation number={78} />, which often flows to WWTPs<Citation number={91} /> or surface water<Citation number={116} />. PETases can treat this leachate before it is discharged. Municipal waste authorities and environmental engineering firms that manage leachate treatment are the buyers in this segment.

**Filtration.** PETases built into filtration cartridges or post-wash modules can degrade microfibres where the filter captures them<Citation number={64} /><Citation number={79} />, before the filter is cleaned or thrown away. Customers include appliance manufacturers and water treatment technology providers that develop household, industrial and municipal filtration systems.

Noticeably, for filtration in particular, regulation is pushing this market forward. In the United States, the proposed Fighting Fibers Act of 2025 would require a microfibre filtration system in every new washing machine sold<Citation number={24} />. As filters become standard, manufacturers will need a way to deal with the microfibres those filters collect<Citation number={112} /><Citation number={122} />. PetaScale plans to supply the enzymes that break down captured PET.

## 7. Business Model

PetaScale is a B2B enzyme company. We engineer plastic-degrading enzymes to work under the conditions each customer already operates in.

Fortunately, published work shows this is achievable. Researchers have found PET-degrading enzymes in compost, marine, and soil environments<Citation number={1} /><Citation number={107} />{/* TODO: the original text also cited Lu et al., 2022, which is not in any reference list provided. Add it as a new numbered reference and a second <Citation /> here once the full citation is confirmed. */}. Our PETadex database gives us 300M unique proteins, so each engineered design begins from an enzyme that already works in conditions close to the customer's.

### How PetaScale Works

1. **Defining the conditions.** The customer provides the operating conditions under which our enzymes must function: parameters such as temperature, pH, and salinity. These inputs define the design environment specifications for enzyme development.
2. **Engineering the enzyme.** We select the closest candidate from PETadex, engineer it to the specification, and validate its activity under the described customer's conditions.
3. **Building the delivery system.** We produce the enzyme and package it in the format its environment requires.
4. **Deploy.** The format depends on the vertical:
   - **WWTPs:** a packed-bed reactor holding immobilized enzymes, installed on a filtered side stream so it adds to existing infrastructure without interrupting plant flow.
   - **Textile effluent and landfill leachate:** metered enzyme dosing into existing treatment lines.
   - **Filtration:** enzyme loaded into cartridges and post-wash modules.

### How We Make Money

- **WWTPs:** Utilities pay per million litres treated under multi-year contracts with regional authorities. The fee covers reactor installation, maintenance, replacement of spent enzyme cartridges, and delivery. Revenue recurs and grows with the volume of water treated.
- **Textile effluent and landfill leachate:** Operators pay for a pilot, which converts to a per-site service contract once we prove performance.
- **Filtration:** Manufacturers license the enzyme or buy enzyme-loaded cartridges as a component, and they keep control of their product and distribution.

### Why This Model Works

We start with WWTPs because a small number of regional authorities control most of the treated water in a region, which gives us recurring revenue and field data early. Each new vertical reuses the same four-step pipeline under different conditions, so expansion costs far less than entering each market from scratch. Every deployment also returns activity data from real operating conditions, and that data shortens the next design cycle.

## 8. Selling & Customer Relationships

The Business Model section sets out what our customers pay for. This section explains how we acquire those customers and retain them.

As aforementioned, our first customers run wastewater treatment plants (WWTPs), which are regulated public infrastructure. An operator's first responsibility is keeping effluent within permit limits, and few operators will change a working process because a new technology sounds promising<Citation number={39} />. For that reason, every sale starts with data from the customer's specified environmental conditions. We ask for a commitment only after the customer has seen our enzymes function within their respective use-case.

### How We Reach Decision-Makers

Our sales process starts with feasibility conversations. Over the iGEM cycle, we spoke with 20 WWTP operators, engineers, researchers, and managers in Ontario, Alberta, and Newfoundland. Through these conversations we learned how plants run day to day, collected data on conditions that would affect enzyme performance, and who approves spending. The conversations also gave us our first working relationships with the staff who would run a pilot.

A single purchase at a municipal plant involves several people and each one needs different evidence. Operators and engineers want to know whether the enzyme will work in their plant, so we show them activity data measured under their own operating conditions. Infrastructure directors and compliance officers focus on risk. For them, we explain how a contained packed-bed reactor fits within existing permits and budgets, and how it prepares the plant for future microplastic monitoring requirements. Procurement officers manage the purchase itself. Public contracts usually go to competitive tender, so our pilot results help define the technical requirements for that tender.

Outside the municipal sector, our buyers include textile sustainability officers, landfill engineers and filtration product managers. These buyers weigh cost against compliance, so we begin with a contained, closed-loop trial on their own waste stream.

### From Feasibility Conversation to Contract

A feasibility conversation converts into a contract in four steps:

1. **Sample collection.** We ask the customer for a sample of their material, such as thickened sludge, along with its temperature, pH and salinity. A customer who sends a sample and assigns staff time to the project has made a real commitment, and we treat that as the first sign of fit.
2. **Laboratory testing.** Before testing begins, the customer sets the success criteria. We then test our enzymes on the sample under the customer's conditions and measure PET degradation using methods both parties have agreed on.
3. **Results report.** We report how much PET the enzyme degraded, how quickly and at what dose. If the results meet the agreed criteria, the customer has evidence drawn from their own material.
4. **Pilot and contract.** A successful test leads to a pilot agreement, under which we install a packed-bed reactor at one plant. If the pilot meets its targets, it converts to a multi-year contract. We write our WWTP contracts to cover every eligible plant a regional authority runs, so a single pilot can open several plants.

We have completed the feasibility stage in the WWTP vertical, and we will use the same process for textile effluent, landfill leachate and filtration.

### What Product-Market Fit Looks Like

We judge fit by what customers commit, since positive feedback costs a customer nothing. In each vertical, we look for a specific commitment:

- **WWTPs:** A regional authority provides sludge samples, signs a pilot agreement with defined success criteria, and budgets for a full contract once the pilot meets those criteria.
- **Textile effluent:** A mill provides production wastewater samples and co-funds an in-line trial, and it names a sustainability or production lead who is accountable for the result.
- **Landfill leachate:** A waste authority or engineering firm provides leachate samples, shares its treatment data and commits a site to a contained pilot.
- **Filtration:** An appliance or filtration manufacturer signs a joint development agreement to test enzyme-loaded cartridges in its own product.

### How We Retain Customers

After a contract is signed, we report PET degradation and system performance to each customer on a fixed schedule, using the testing methods agreed at the start. Regular reporting keeps results visible to the decision‑makers who approved the purchase, which is very critical when contracts come up for renewal. Each deployment also feeds field data back into our engineering pipeline, enabling us to design higher‑performing enzymes for that customer and others within adjacent verticals.

## 11. IP Strategy and Administration

This section sets out how PetaScale protects its technology, structures itself as a company, and manages financial, legal and operational risk as it moves from research into commercial deployment.

### IP Strategy

**PetaScale’s primary protected asset is its engineered enzymes.**

Each patent filing claims a specific engineered sequence, the mutations introduced into a natural starting enzyme, along with closely related variants. Engineered proteins are patentable, whereas naturally occurring sequences generally are not; our protection therefore rests on the modifications we make rather than on the natural candidates in the PETadex. As our customer base expands, our IP portfolio grows in parallel, allowing the business and its intellectual property to scale together.

A patent protects one enzyme for a fixed term. Our pipeline and accumulating field data enable us to design the next enzyme faster than competitors can engineer around the last.

#### IP Assignment and the Open-Source Boundary

PetaBite grew out of a large student team, so we will complete a full intellectual property assignment sweep before incorporation, while contributors are still reachable. Every member who worked on the design pipeline, the enzyme candidates or the bioreactor hardware signs an assignment to the company, including members who have graduated.

We will also document where the boundary sits between the open-source PETadex release and the proprietary work PetaScale owns. While the PETadex remains open to all researchers, the engineered enzymes belong to the company. Recording this boundary in writing, with contributor attribution on each side, prevents the ownership ambiguity that commonly delays university spinout financings.

### Corporate Structure

We will incorporate federally under the Canada Business Corporations Act in March 2027 as a single Canadian parent company. This keeps PetaScale a Canadian-Controlled Private Corporation (CCPC), which carries two financial benefits:

- **Refundable SR&ED credit.** A CCPC claims an enhanced 35% credit on qualified R&D expenditures, refundable in cash even when the company owes no tax. A foreign-controlled subsidiary falls to a 15% credit that is generally non-refundable and returns little before profitability. On our projected R&D spend, this is our largest source of non-dilutive funding through 2030.
- **Lifetime Capital Gains Exemption.** Canadian founders can each shelter up to $1.25 million of capital gains at an exit, provided the company remains Canadian-controlled and its assets stay in active use in Canada.

Canadian control also preserves our eligibility for federal and provincial grant programs that restrict funding to Canadian-controlled companies, and it keeps CCPC stock option treatment available to Canadian employees, who can defer tax until they sell rather than when they exercise.

We will add a Delaware subsidiary when we sign our first United States customer, which lets us contract and hire there while the parent keeps its status. We will reassess the parent structure at Series A, since many United States venture funds invest only in a Delaware C Corporation. Restructuring to a Delaware parent would end CCPC status and the benefits above, and exchanging Canadian shares for shares of a United States parent is generally a taxable event for shareholders at fair market value with no cash attached. We will price that restructuring against what we would give up before we act, and we negotiate our University of Toronto licence to permit assignment to a future parent so the option stays open.

#### Cap Table and Governance

We will establish a single cap table at incorporation with one class of common shares, four-year founder vesting on a one-year cliff, and a documented option pool. We keep early capital in a single instrument rather than a series of differently structured agreements, and we document any equity discussed with advisors or early contributors in writing.

Our board forms when our first financing round closes. Until then, founder decisions above the thresholds in Financial Controls require agreement from two founders.

### Financial Controls

PetaScale will operate under written financial controls from incorporation. Clear controls prevent overspending, reduce fraud risk, and produce the records investors expect during diligence.

- **Spending limits.** Before our first round closes, expenses above $5,000 require approval from two founders. Once the board is constituted, expenses above $5,000 require board approval, and we review the threshold annually as the operating budget grows.
- **Approval workflows.** We define who signs off on expenses, contracts, vendor payments and purchases. Medium-sized expenses require dual approval, and major commitments, including multi-year supplier agreements and capital purchases for the bioreactor array, require board approval. No single person can authorize a large or sensitive financial action alone.
- **Audit procedures.** We reconcile bank statements, expenses and accounting entries monthly. An external accountant reviews our records annually. We maintain documented standards for file naming, storage and retention, which supports both investor diligence and our annual SR&ED claim.

Our SR&ED claim depends on contemporaneous records, so we log R&D time, experimental hypotheses and results as the work happens rather than reconstructing them at year end.

### Insurance

We will carry general coverage from incorporation and deployment-specific coverage before our first pilot installation.

- **General liability.** Covers physical injury, property damage and accidents arising from our operations, field testing and pilot deployments.
- **Directors and officers (D&O).** Protects founders and board members against personal liability for management decisions and fiduciary duties. We put this in place when outside investors join and the board is constituted.
- **Intellectual property liability.** Covers legal costs if another party claims infringement, and the cost of enforcing our own filings. Our portfolio is central to our defensibility, so this protects the asset the company is built on.

**Coverage specific to a biological product.** Deploying engineered enzymes creates several exposures we must account for:

- **Environmental liability.** Covers accidental release, contamination or unintended ecological effects. Fortunately, our contained packed-bed reactor design keeps this risk very low, but insurers expect the category to be covered for any biological product operating in a water system.
- **Product liability.** Covers harm caused if an enzyme performs differently than expected or interacts with environmental substrates in an unintended way.
- **Regulatory compliance risk.** Covers the cost of regulatory investigations or enforcement actions from environmental regulators, wastewater authorities or biosafety agencies.

### Regulatory and Biosafety Compliance

Our Human Practices and Entrepreneurship team has mapped the pathways our deployments fall under, including the Canadian Environmental Protection Act<Citation number={2} />, EPA TSCA Section 5 in the United States<Citation number={3} />, and provincial discharge permits. We will lead with a contained packed-bed reactor so our deployments are classified as contained use rather than open environmental release<Citation number={49} />, which is the lower regulatory burden and the safer starting position.

We will prioritize enzyme candidates with close homology to naturally occurring proteins, and we maintain a documented risk framework that sets the ecological safety evidence we require before any deployment. We will engage regulators at Environment and Climate Change Canada and the United States EPA ahead of our pilots.

### Data and Confidentiality

Customer samples, operating conditions and field performance data are commercially sensitive to the customer and central to our own pipeline. We will handle both under written agreements: customers sign a mutual non-disclosure agreement before providing samples, and our service contracts specify what data we may retain and use to improve future enzyme designs. Every employee and contractor signs confidentiality and IP assignment terms as a condition of engagement.

#### Incubator Programs

**1. Wilbe Venture Incubator: Sprint Completion**

In April 2026, we completed Wilbe's venture build sprint. Wilbe is an international venture platform for scientist-founders that offers business training, community, lab access and first-cheque capital. Its online cohorts help researchers turn an idea into an investment-ready venture case. The sprint produced an investment-readiness review of PetaScale with two main recommendations, and we have acted on both:

- **Sharpen the value proposition.** Replace our platform description with one sentence about customer outcomes that a non-expert, such as a wastewater operator, understands immediately. The review identified this as the change with the highest near-term return.
- **Treat wastewater as the wedge.** Build stronger evidence on customer willingness to pay and specific adoption triggers, and define the path from wastewater into adjacent markets.

**What we learned:** The review identified the clarity of our commercial story as our biggest gap. Both recommendations pointed to the same goal, making PetaScale easy for a non‑expert customer to understand.
